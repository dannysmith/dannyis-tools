Glossary

The running rigging of a 74-gun ship, about 1805

Every term of art used in this tutorial, in the form the tutorial uses, with the spellings the period books print. Where Steel, Lever or Falconer defines a word, their definition is quoted. Words that came into use after 1815 are marked.

Three books supply most of the quotations: David Steel’s Elements and Practice of Rigging and Seamanship (1794), Darcy Lever’s Young Sea Officer’s Sheet Anchor (1808, quoted from the 1819 text) and William Falconer’s Universal Dictionary of the Marine (1769). They do not spell alike. Steel writes clue-line, haliard, jears, tye, parral, laniard and pendent; Lever writes clew-line, halliards, jears, tye and parral; Falconer writes clue-line, haliards, jears, parrel and tye. The tutorial uses one form of each and gives the others here.

A definition with no quotation is in the tutorial’s own words, from how the period books use the term. The few that rest on general knowledge say so.
