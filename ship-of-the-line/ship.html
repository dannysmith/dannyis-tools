The ship

Spars, sails and standing rigging of a 74-gun ship, about 1805

You know most of these names. The drawing is here to be flipped back to: every spar and sail, and how often each sail was really set. Below it, the standing rigging in order, with the reasons for it.

    The spars, with Steel’s dimensions

    From David Steel’s table of 1794, “74 guns, 1799 tons”. His 74 is a large-class ship with a lower deck of 176 feet; a common 74 of the Bellona kind, about 168 feet, would have spars a foot or two shorter throughout. Lengths are reliable. The fractions in the diameters were read from a poor scan and a few may be a quarter of an inch out. A row picks the spar out in the drawing.

    The sails, by mast

    Thirty-two sails, of which about a dozen did the ordinary work. The grading is from Steel (1794), Lever (1808) and Falconer (1769). Dates of introduction and withdrawal are mostly from James Lees’s chronology, which was seen only in snippets and not read in full, so treat them as his and unchecked.

    How this is drawn, and how far to trust it

    The spars are those of the 74 in David Steel’s tables of 1794, and the mast positions follow his rule. Heights above the water are estimates, good to a few feet. Where a rope comes down on the channel, which side of a mast a stay passes, and where a fall is made fast on deck are mostly not given by the sources; those are drawn dashed or said to be guesses in the text.

    Steel’s running text describes a 20-gun ship; the 74 comes from his tables, which give ropes, sizes and numbers but not leads. Where the two layers disagree, as over the number of shrouds, the text here says so.

    Dates of introduction and withdrawal come mostly from James Lees, The Masting and Rigging of English Ships of War 1625–1860. That book was reached only as search snippets. Each date is his, at second hand, and is marked as such where it is used.

    The ship is rigged as of about 1805: a gaff and not a mizen yard, a brailed mizen with a driver set over it in fair weather, a dolphin striker, a flying jibboom, royals set flying. In 1794 the first four of those would have been different.
